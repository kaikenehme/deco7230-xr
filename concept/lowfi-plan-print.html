XR Renovation Previewer Low-fidelity prototype and test plan

Kaike Nehme · 7 August 2026 · Digital Prototyping and Extended Reality

01The concept

Phone apps for previewing a renovation — Dulux Visualizer, Houzz, IKEA Kreativ — all fail the same way. They show a candidate colour as a flat rectangle on a six-inch screen, lit by a generic renderer, detached from the room it is meant to go in. The three things that actually decide whether a renovation works are all spatial: scale, adjacency to what you are keeping, and light.

This project is a phone paint-visualiser app, but using XR so you can hold a sample up against the surfaces you are keeping, at full size, in your own light. You pull samples directly off the things staying in the room — the floor, the sofa — and those samples generate what works with them. Nothing is picked from a catalogue: the room you already have decides what fits.
The one part being prototyped today
Pull a sample off something you're keeping  →  hold it up against something you're changing  →  rotate to vary it  →  release to commit
Not tested today: scheme comparison · marking keep/change · MR passthrough · colour-harmony maths · anything involving the headset.

02The physical prototype

Plate 1 — front. Two hinged paper walls, one scribbled red and one green. Card and playdoh furniture on a paper floor.
Plate 2 — eye level. Viewed from inside the room, at the height a seated occupant would judge the space from.

What it is made of

Red wallchangeable Paper panel, hand-scribbled. Lifts off and is swapped by the wizard to simulate a colour change.
Green wallchangeable Second panel at right angles, so two candidate colours can be judged in one glance.
Card armchairkept Left as bare kraft card — uncoloured, so it reads as the existing furniture that is staying. The sample source.
Playdoh sofachangeable Moulded by hand. Colour deliberately matched to the green wall — tests whether the loop reads the same on furniture as on walls.
Card tablechangeable Corrugated card, scribbled red to match the red wall. The second half of the coordination test.
LED tea lights ×2light Warm point sources at floor level, against cool ambient room light.

What emerged from building it

Light
The tea lights were not planned, and they are the most valuable thing here. In both plates the red wall reads warm orange near the flame and cool pink further away — the same paper, two apparent colours. This is precisely the affordance the concept claims XR has over a phone, demonstrated in cardboard before a line of code exists.
Matching
Coordination happened by hand, unprompted. The sofa was matched to the green wall and the table to the red one while building. The instinct the concept is designed around turned up on its own, which is weak but real evidence that the premise is sound.
Materials
Three materials, three jobs. Paper = the changeable surface. Card = the fixed structure. Playdoh = form that can be reshaped as well as recoloured, which raises a question the digital version has not answered: should furniture be re-shapeable, or only recolourable?
ADD BEFORE TESTING — the card armchair must be visibly labelled as staying. If nothing marks it, participants cannot discover a rule the prototype does not express, and question Q1 below returns a false negative. A strip of tape or a written label is enough.

03The interaction being tested

STEP 01
Pull
Touch a surface you are keeping — the bare card armchair — and draw your hand away. A sample of its colour comes with you.
STEP 02
Hold up
Raise the sample toward a surface you want to change. As it nears, that surface previews the colour behind it.
STEP 03
Tune
Rotate the sample in your hand to move through the range that works with the thing you pulled it from. Warmer, cooler, deeper.
STEP 04
Commit
Release. The preview becomes the surface. Move away without releasing and it reverts.

There are no menus anywhere in that loop. Every change is a physical act on the thing being changed — a direct response to the brief's warning that XR interactions must not be "limited to simply pushing VR buttons".

04What we need to find out

QuestionIf the answer is no
Q1Do people work out that samples come from the things being kept?Kept surfaces need a visual affordance
Q2Is holding a sample near a surface understood as "preview"?Proximity-preview fails; needs an explicit trigger
Q3Do people rotate the sample to ask for a variation?Twist-to-tune is arbitrary — cut it or redesign it
Q4Does the same gesture read correctly for furniture as for walls?The loop does not generalise; scope is at risk
Q5Do fewer, constrained options feel helpful or restrictive?The core premise is wrong

Assumptions under challenge

A1Discoverability. The loop can be performed without instruction.
A2Constraint as help. Generating options from what is staying reduces choice paralysis rather than causing frustration.

Q5 / A2 matters most. It can genuinely fail, and a failure is a finding worth reporting — not a problem to hide.

05Methodology

Wizard-of-Oz play-acting on a physical low-fidelity prototype. A second person acts as the system: when the participant performs a gesture, the wizard physically swaps the paper to produce what the app would have done. The participant is never told how it works. This tests the interaction model before any code exists — and discoverability cannot be measured if you explain the interface first.

Participant
Uses the prototype. Thinks aloud throughout.
Wizard
Silently swaps paper per the rules. Never explains.
Observer
Times, tallies, records verbatim quotes. Says nothing.

Wizard response rules — follow exactly, every run

Participant doesWizard does
Holds a swatch near a surfaceSwap that surface's panel to the swatch colour. Hold it there.
Moves the swatch awayPut the original panel back.
Rotates the swatch in handHand over a different swatch from the same harmony set.
Releases it onto a surfaceLeave the new panel in place — committed.
Taps or presses a surfaceDo nothing. Observer records it.
Touches the kept armchairHand them a swatch matching its colour.
Tries to sample a changeable surfaceDo nothing. Observer records it.
Asks how it works"What would you expect?" Nothing more.

06Step-by-step testing process

One run ≈ 6 min 30 s. Aim for three to four runs. Reset the room to its starting state between participants and photograph it, so everyone begins identically.

TimeStepScript and what is observed
0:30SetupRoom on table, kept item labelled, swatch deck to one side. Reset any previous changes.
0:30Brief"This is a living room. The armchair is staying. Everything else can change. Think out loud." Nothing about how it works.
1:30Free explorationNo task given. Observe first actions, whether they touch the kept item, whether they try to tap. → D1, D2, D4, D6
2:00Task 1 — colour"Change the room so it works with the armchair you're keeping." Wizard responds per rules. → D3, D5, D7
1:00Task 2 — furniture"Now change a piece of furniture the same way." Tests Q4.
1:00Post-taskD8, then: "What did you think would happen when you held the card up?" and "Where did you think the colours came from?"

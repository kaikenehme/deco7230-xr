01Objective and validation metrics

Category: Creation

Interactive Prototype 1 is a VR living room built in Unity 6 with the XR Interaction Toolkit and driven by controllers. The floor and sofa are staying; everything else can change. The concept turns on one hero interaction, the hold-up loop: touch a kept surface and a colour sample comes off it, hold that sample against a wall to preview a harmonised paint, twist your wrist to tune it, and let go to commit. A conventional controller menu sits alongside it for comparison, carrying a 24-colour Dulux catalogue, floor materials and eight pieces of furniture. Two secondary diegetic controls round the room out: a lamp that cycles warm, cool and daylight when touched, and a scheme save and cycle.

The testing plan set out three assumptions. A2 was the primary one, because the whole concept rests on it and it could genuinely fail.

The plan fixed the measures and the 20-second prompt rule but never set numeric pass marks. Rather than invent thresholds after the fact, I judged each aim on the direction and consistency of the evidence across participants, and I say so openly in §4. Each session ran for about five minutes: a short brief, thirty seconds of uninstructed free look, Task 1 ("repaint this room so it works with the floor and sofa you're keeping"), Task 2 (change the floor, add and move furniture, save and switch schemes), then four post-test questions. One paper sheet per participant, sixteen rows; the sheet itself is in Appendix D.

Setting, read this before the results. The studio session ran on the XR Device Simulator inside the Unity Editor on a laptop, with mouse and keyboard standing in for the headset and controllers (grip = G, trigger = click, wrist twist = mouse-driven controller rotation). The borrowed Quest refused to authorise over USB, so the built APK was never sideloaded. Every "controller" action below was really a mouse action. That confounds every discoverability measure, and I treat it that way throughout.

Data quality. The studio sheets captured 11 and 10 of their 16 rows; the supplementary sheets captured 15 each. Nobody's sheet records which of the six scripted prompts was used, only how many. 03Analysis and insights

Five participants across two settings is a small sample. I report counts rather than percentages, and I only call something a pattern when it held for every participant it applied to.

The clearest result is that the loop's entry gesture happens and the loop never starts. All five participants touched the floor or the sofa during free look, which is exactly the gesture that begins the hold-up loop, and none of them went on to pull a sample. Touching a kept surface simply gives no response that reads as "you can take this". The result survived a change of setting, a facilitator who had already run the protocol, and participants who probably knew what the project was, so I trust it as far as five people can be trusted. I read it as a failure of affordance rather than of the concept: the interaction was never reached, so it was never judged.

The menu won every time, but by default rather than by preference. Both routes to a paint choice were invisible. The menu produced all five kept choices only because the fifth scripted prompt ("point at the floor and press the trigger") reveals it directly, while nothing in the room reveals the hold-up loop. Of the three sheets that record how the menu was reached, all three say it took a prompt. So this is a discoverability result, and I do not read it as people preferring menus.

Prompting stayed constant at roughly one intervention per minute, 5 and 3 in the studio and then 4, 2 and 4 at home, against a 20-second stuck rule. Practice and a familiar setting changed nothing, which points at the prototype rather than at the sessions.

Everything participants found confusing was about manipulation, not choice. The confusing thing was the controller (three times), drag and drop (twice), the selection highlight (twice), or rotating furniture, and P2 and P4 independently said almost the same sentence: selecting furniture should place it, not drop it at your feet. Nobody was confused by the colours, the options or the room. Some of this is simulator cost, a mouse standing in for a hand, but place versus drop and the highlight are prototype decisions and would look the same on a headset.

The diegetic lamp can be found, just not in the studio. Two flatmates touched it unprompted; neither studio participant did. The data cannot separate a practised facilitator, prior exposure to the project and a more relaxed free look, so I leave the why open. The scheme save and cycle was found by nobody in either setting.

Opinions on the limited options split once n grew, and they were about the wrong thing. The studio pair said "it's good" and "enough for now"; at home I got "feels good for now" but also "feels a bit restrictive" and "need more painting options". All five were rating the unconstrained 24-colour catalogue. That says nothing about A2 directly, but it is a warning: if 24 free colours already feel thin to two people out of five, a seven-option harmony set will read as fewer unless the constraint visibly explains itself.

06Reflection and next steps

What worked. The uninstructed phase did its job. It produced an unambiguous negative on the entry gesture that a guided demo would have hidden, and keeping "did" separate from "said" on the sheet made the pattern in §3 easy to see. Adding three real supplementary sessions was cheap, and it turned a 2 out of 2 observation into a 5 out of 5 one without changing a single verdict, which is itself worth knowing.

What didn't. The platform. A body interaction was tested through a mouse, five times, because the Quest would not authorise over USB on the day. The same pipeline risk hangs over IP2b, so it gets solved first rather than last. Facilitating alone with a paper tally lost about a third of the studio rows; at home, with practice, it lost almost none, but the same instrument gaps came back because I never revised the sheet between rounds.

Instrument. The IP2a sheet gets three fixes. Row 10 branches, so the constraint question is only asked of people who actually used the constrained set, and menu users are asked why the menu instead. Row 11 is reworded so it stops collecting "what was hard". And the trusted-method question gets printed on the sheet instead of living in the plan. Beyond those, prompt numbers rather than ticks go into the tally boxes so I know which prompt unstuck whom, and sessions are screen recorded with consent so lost rows can be recovered.

Sample. Flatmates got n to five, but they bring prior exposure, which may explain the lamp result. Next round I recruit outside the flat, log whether the participant has seen the project before, and budget seven minutes per participant rather than five, because setup and briefing ate the studio's time.

Next. IP2a runs on the headset, with the entry affordance rebuilt, Task 1 starting sample in hand, and A2, still the primary aim, finally measured.
